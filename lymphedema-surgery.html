Lymphedema surgery

Microsurgical treatment for chronic limb swelling caused by lymphatic dysfunction — most often after cancer treatment. The flagship consultancy at St. Luke's BGC.

What it is

When lymphatic channels are blocked or removed, fluid that should drain away collects in the limb. Compression and therapy manage that; surgery attempts to restore an outflow.

Two microsurgical approaches are used. Lymphaticovenous anastomosis joins blocked lymphatic channels directly to nearby small veins, giving the fluid a route out — the vessels involved are often well under a millimetre across. Vascularised lymph node transfer moves healthy lymph nodes, with their blood supply, into the affected limb. Which is appropriate depends on stage and on what imaging shows, assessed with indocyanine green lymphography and high-frequency ultrasound.

Clinical Overview

Understanding Lymphedema

I
How Common It Is
Lymphedema affects an estimated 250 million people worldwide. Around the world, the most common cause is a parasitic infection called lymphatic filariasis — but in a surgical practice like this one, the more familiar story is lymphedema that develops after cancer treatment. It shows up most often after breast cancer surgery: roughly 20–30% of patients who have lymph nodes removed from the armpit go on to develop it, and even the less invasive sentinel node biopsy carries a smaller, real risk of around 5–7%. Radiation to the area raises that risk further. Cancer surgery involving the pelvis, urinary tract, or head and neck can lead to lymphedema there too, for the same underlying reason.
II
What Causes It
Some people are simply born with a lymphatic system that doesn’t work quite right — this is called primary lymphedema, and it’s uncommon. Depending on the type, it can show up at birth, around puberty, or later in adulthood. Far more often, lymphedema is secondary, meaning something damaged lymphatics that used to work fine. The usual causes are cancer surgery, radiation, injury, repeated infections, or long-standing vein problems that eventually affect the lymphatics too. Worldwide, the single biggest cause is filarial infection, though that’s rarely what brings a patient to this clinic.
III
What’s Happening in the Limb
Normally, the lymphatic system quietly drains protein-rich fluid out of the tissues and back into the bloodstream. When that system is overwhelmed — too few working channels, faulty valves, or a blockage — the fluid has nowhere to go and builds up under the skin. Left alone, that ongoing buildup causes inflammation, and over time the tissue itself starts to change: soft, reversible swelling can gradually turn into firmer, fibrous tissue that’s much harder to treat. This is really the whole reason timing matters — the earlier lymphedema is addressed, surgically or not, the better and more lasting the results tend to be.

IV
Non-Surgical Care
Before surgery is ever on the table — and often alongside it — the first line of treatment is complete decongestive therapy. It runs in two stages: an intensive stretch of hands-on lymphatic drainage massage, snug compression bandaging, skin care and guided exercise, followed by an ongoing maintenance phase built around a properly fitted compression garment, self-massage and staying active. Pneumatic compression devices can help at home between visits. Skin and nail care matter more than people expect — an infection can set back months of progress, and damaged lymphatics make infection more likely in the first place, so it’s worth breaking that cycle early. Keeping weight in a healthy range helps too, since extra weight measurably makes lymphedema worse.
V
Surgical Options
When surgery makes sense, it generally falls into one of two categories. The first restores function: lymphaticovenous anastomosis reroutes blocked lymphatic channels into nearby tiny veins, giving fluid a new way out, and works best when some lymphatic function is still intact. Vascularised lymph node transfer takes healthy lymph nodes, with their own blood supply, and moves them into the affected area — this can sometimes be combined with breast reconstruction for appropriate breast cancer patients. The second category focuses on reducing the fibrous, fatty tissue that builds up once the condition is more advanced — mainly through targeted liposuction, with more extensive excisional surgery reserved for the most severe, long-standing cases. Which approach fits is decided after imaging — indocyanine green lymphography and high-frequency ultrasound — maps out exactly how the lymphatics are functioning.
